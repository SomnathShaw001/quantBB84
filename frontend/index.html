The BB84 Quantum Key Distribution Protocol Architecture

Developed by Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard in 1984, BB84 establishes information-theoretic secret keys guaranteed by the fundamental laws of quantum mechanics.

Phase 1: Quantum State Preparation & Transmission
Alice generates uniform random classical bits $b_A \in \{0, 1\}$ and uniform random bases $a \in \{Z, X\}$. Qubits are encoded using single photons:
  • Z (Rectilinear +): $0 \rightarrow |0\rangle$, $1 \rightarrow |1\rangle$
  • X (Diagonal ×): $0 \rightarrow |+\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle + |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}$, $1 \rightarrow |-\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle - |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}$
Phase 2: The No-Cloning Theorem & Eve's Disturbance
Wootters and Zurek (1982) proved that an unknown quantum state cannot be cloned identically. When Eve attempts an intercept-resend attack:
  1. Eve must choose a measurement basis without knowing Alice's basis.
  2. Eve picks the wrong basis 50% of the time ($P(\text{basis mismatch}) = 0.5$).
  3. When Eve's basis matches Alice's, state is measured deterministically ($0\%$ error).
  4. When Eve's basis mismatches, measurement collapses the state into her basis. When Bob subsequently measures in Alice's basis, Bob experiences a 50% error probability.
  5. Overall expected QBER on basis-matched qubits: $Q = 0.5 \times 0.5 = 25\%$.
Phase 3: Sifting, Error Correction & Privacy Amplification

Over a classical authenticated public channel, Alice and Bob exchange their chosen bases and discard all positions where their bases mismatched (Sifting, retaining ~50% of transmitted bits).

A random subset of sifted bits are publicly sacrificed to measure the Quantum Bit Error Rate (QBER). If $Q > 11\%$ (Shor-Preskill security bound), the protocol immediately ABORTS.

If $Q \le 11\%$, remaining bits undergo Cascade Error Correction to eliminate channel noise, followed by Toeplitz Universal Hashing (Privacy Amplification) to compress the key and eliminate any partial information leaked to Eve.

Academic Examination & Viva Voce Q&A Reference

Q1: Why does Eve introduce an error rate of exactly 25%?
Alice and Bob only compare sifted positions where Alice and Bob chose the SAME basis. Eve chooses her basis randomly. In 50% of cases, Eve guesses correctly and preserves the state. In the remaining 50%, Eve chooses the conjugate basis, collapsing the state into a superposition relative to Bob's basis. Bob then measures a random bit with 50% probability of error. Hence, total QBER = $0.5 \times 0 + 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$ or 25%.
Q2: Why is the threshold set to 11% instead of 25%?
The Shor-Preskill asymptotic bound for BB84 shows that positive secret key rate is achievable only when $1 - 2 h_2(Q) > 0$, where $h_2(Q)$ is the binary Shannon entropy. Solving $h_2(Q) = 0.5$ yields $Q \approx 11.0\%$. Above 11%, the mutual information between Alice and Bob drops below the mutual information between Alice and Eve, rendering privacy amplification mathematically unable to extract a secret key.
Q3: Does QBER = 0 prove 100% cryptographic security?
No. In finite-length key exchange, statistical fluctuation can conceal an adversary who intercepts only a tiny fraction of qubits. Furthermore, physical side-channel attacks (detector blinding, Trojan horse pulses, photon-number-splitting on multi-photon pulses) can leak key material without raising the qubit error rate.
Q4: Limitations of this software simulator
This simulator runs on a local Clifford stabilizer simulator (Qiskit Aer). It models single-photon BB84, depolarizing noise, bit-flip noise, and photon loss. It does not model continuous-variable QKD, detector dark count rates, phase drift, finite-key composable security bounds, or photon-number splitting (PNS) attacks on attenuated coherent states.
